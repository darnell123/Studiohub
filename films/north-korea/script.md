## The Workers Behind the Names

Company laptops sit inside a home in Arizona. They belong to employers elsewhere, but this is where the machines stay connected.

The woman hosting them is Christina Chapman, an American who helps overseas programmers work remotely through computers in the United States.

According to federal authorities, the operation reaches more than 300 American businesses. Its clients include major corporations. More than $17 million passes through the scheme.

To an employer, a connected laptop can look like an employee working from home. Behind that connection, authorities say, are North Korean workers using other people’s identities.

But right now, in April 2024, investigators are closing in on Chapman’s address. The companies have supplied the equipment and paid the wages. Who have they actually hired—and where has their money gone?

This story is pieced together from court records, government advisories, company disclosures and news reporting.

The programmers are real. The identities are the fiction.

Before Christina Chapman’s home becomes part of this story, North Korea already has an overseas software business. Its workers build applications, write code and compete for contracts. Their customers believe they are hiring people from somewhere else.

Two companies sit near the beginning of the trail: Yanbian Silverstar Network Technology in China, and Volasys Silverstar in Russia. American prosecutors later describe both as North Korean-controlled fronts, employing or retaining at least 130 North Korean IT workers.

Inside that network, prosecutors say, the workers have a name: “IT Warriors.”

The alleged conspiracy stretches from April 2017 to March 2023. It includes company leaders, managers and programmers, operating across borders while pursuing work from American businesses and organizations.

In 2018, the United States sanctions both companies. But a sanctioned company name is only one identity in a business built around replacing identities.

A worker can approach a client through a freelance profile instead. A different name. A different employment history. A different country.

By August 2019, an American tells the FBI that someone later identified as a North Korean IT worker has used their U.S.-based freelance account.

That account supplies something the programmer’s skills cannot: an apparently acceptable identity at the entrance to the job market.

According to U.S. authorities, North Korea sends these workers overseas to earn foreign currency and get around sanctions. Many operate from China and Russia; others work from parts of Africa and Southeast Asia.

Their products can be completely ordinary. A mobile application. A website. A piece of business software.

The destination of the pay is not.

U.S. authorities say the revenue supports the North Korean government, including its weapons programs. Treasury estimates that the state can withhold as much as 90 percent of overseas workers’ wages.

The workers themselves can live under close supervision. North Korean defector Hyun-Seung Lee later describes shared apartments, long hours and restrictions on going outside alone. In his account, a team leader controls the outings.

For the client, the relationship looks like remote contracting. For the worker, according to that account, even leaving the apartment is not an individual decision.

There is still a practical obstacle. A profile can claim to be American. But what happens when an American employer wants to send its new employee a computer?

## An American Address

Christina Chapman enters the operation around October 2020, according to the Justice Department’s later account.

Her contribution is physical. She has a home in Arizona, an American internet connection and space for employers’ computers.

Those computers become the bridge.

The arrangement works in three parts.

First, the applicant gets hired under a borrowed, stolen or fabricated identity. The employer sends its laptop to a U.S. address.

Second, a facilitator receives the machine and enables remote access. That can mean installing software, or connecting hardware that allows someone elsewhere to control the keyboard, screen and mouse.

Third, the overseas worker operates the computer. The employer’s own equipment remains connected inside the United States.

The laptop is where the company expects it to be. The employee is not.

Chapman’s home becomes what authorities call a laptop farm: company-issued machines hosted domestically for people working abroad.

The facilitator can handle more than equipment. The FBI later describes helpers supplying job-platform accounts, financial accounts and company fronts. Some attend interviews or meetings on a worker’s behalf.

There is a human answer available for a question the technology cannot handle.

By May 16, 2022, the FBI, State Department and Treasury issue a joint warning. They say North Korea has dispatched thousands of IT workers around the world, posing as people of other nationalities.

The earnings can be substantial. The agencies estimate that some individuals can make more than $300,000 a year. A team can generate more than $3 million annually.

A worker does not necessarily stop at one employer.

One former North Korean IT worker later says he creates another false profile after landing a job, then pursues a second position. He describes creating 20 to 50 profiles a year until he gets hired.

And some of those hires become valued colleagues.

In summer 2022, according to one company executive’s account, a remote engineer is the team’s most productive member. His coworkers organize a surprise birthday video call.

His response is unexpected: “But it’s not my birthday.”

The company’s security team subsequently finds remote-access tools on his work computer. He is fired. Much later, investigators find one of his pay stubs at Chapman’s Arizona home.

For the colleagues on that call, the person has been present in meetings and productive at work. But a basic detail of his identity does not fit.

Elsewhere, entire teams are preparing answers for exactly that kind of problem.

## The Script and the Search

Thirty pages of interview scripts offer a way through the hiring process.

The documents surface in reporting in November 2023, alongside false résumés, online profiles, interview notes and forged identities discovered by cybersecurity researchers.

One sample answer describes a good corporate culture as a place where people are free to express ideas and opinions.

Another supplies a personal explanation for working from the wrong country. A supposed developer is in Singapore to be with parents after COVID, but plans to return to Los Angeles.

The script provides a family, a journey and a future return date. All the small details that make an unfamiliar applicant sound like an ordinary person with an ordinary life.

According to one report, a single worker has accounts on more than 20 freelance websites, spanning the United States, Britain, Japan, Uzbekistan, Spain, Australia and New Zealand.

The operation also supplies employers that exist mainly to make previous employment look convincing.

In October 2023, the Justice Department announces the seizure of 17 website domains. Authorities say North Korean workers use them to impersonate legitimate American IT-services companies and strengthen fraudulent work histories.

The workers behind that particular network are linked to the two Silverstar companies in China and Russia, officials say.

The money moves through online payment services and Chinese bank accounts, according to the Justice Department. By November, authorities also report approximately $1.5 million seized and thousands of fraudulent freelance and payment accounts shut down through U.S.–South Korean cooperation.

Meanwhile, Chapman is handling the everyday problems of keeping remote employees apparently local.

In reported messages, a worker asks whether she knows how to install AnyDesk, a remote-access application.

“I do it practically EVERYDAY!” she replies.

In another reported exchange, a worker wants her to join a laptop-onboarding meeting, mute herself and follow the employer’s technical instructions.

Chapman asks: “Who do I say I am?”

The job title might belong to a software engineer. The person facing the employer’s support staff is the woman hosting the machine.

By April 2024, investigators reach her Arizona home.

The search uncovers more than 90 laptops, according to the Justice Department.

This is the address from the beginning: not an overseas intelligence facility, but an American home holding computers supplied by employers who believe they are equipping their own workers.

The devices are only one part of what investigators uncover. There are identities, payroll records, financial accounts and shipments overseas.

And Chapman is not the only person selling the missing American pieces.

## A Market for Becoming Someone Else

Upworksell.com offers another route into the same labor market.

Prosecutors say its operator, Ukrainian national Oleksandr Didenko, helps overseas workers buy or rent identities and accounts. In May 2024, U.S. authorities seize the domain, and Polish authorities arrest him.

The Justice Department later says he manages as many as 871 proxy identities and facilitates at least three American laptop farms.

His operation helps North Korean workers obtain employment at 40 U.S. companies, officials say. It also helps clients reach American financial services through money transmitters, without physically opening a U.S. bank account.

In the complaint, one customer asks Didenko to send a laptop from one of his farms to Chapman’s Arizona farm.

Separate operators. A shared customer. A company computer moving between them.

The American cases become public in May, when authorities unseal charges against Chapman and separately charge Didenko.

Then, in July, a cybersecurity company announces that it has hired a North Korean IT worker itself.

The company is KnowBe4, which trains organizations to recognize security threats.

Its applicant has passed multiple video interviews, identity checks and background checks. According to reporting, the new hire is called “Kyle.”

The company says it detects an attempted malware installation on the supplied computer and terminates the worker. It says no systems are breached.

Chief executive Stu Sjouwerman acknowledges that the company has “egg on our face.”

Even a business dedicated to security has completed the hiring process without discovering who is behind the application.

A separate case announced in August reaches Nashville. Prosecutors allege that facilitator Matthew Isaac Knoot receives company laptops addressed to a stolen identity, “Andrew M.,” and installs unauthorized remote-access software.

According to the charges, workers in China then appear to be working from Tennessee. The Justice Department says affected companies incur more than $500,000 auditing and repairing devices, systems and networks.

By December, prosecutors charge 14 North Korean nationals connected to the Silverstar companies.

The indictment alleges a six-year operation generating at least $88 million for North Korea. Some workers are allegedly ordered to earn at least $10,000 a month. The firms allegedly run “socialism competitions” rewarding strong earners.

But salaries are not the only alleged source of money.

Prosecutors say some workers steal proprietary information, then demand payment to keep it private. In some cases, they allegedly publish it.

An employer can end the contract. That does not necessarily end the worker’s leverage.

## Following the Paycheck

Laos appears on the money trail in January 2025.

Treasury sanctions a network that it links to North Korean IT workers and electronics procurement. At its center is Department 53, a weapons-trading organization subordinate to North Korea’s Ministry of National Defense.

According to Treasury, Department 53 uses front companies in several industries, including software development, to generate revenue.

One company, Korea Osong Shipping, has maintained a delegation of IT workers in Laos since at least 2022. Another, Chonsurim Trading, has directed a group there since at least 2021.

Treasury says the workers use aliases and false identification while developing websites, mobile applications and cryptocurrency exchanges. Revenue from one delegation supports North Korea’s munitions sector, officials say.

The route begins with something as ordinary as payroll.

In Chapman’s case, employers deposit wages into American financial accounts. She also receives and forges payroll checks in stolen identities’ names, according to the Justice Department, then transfers proceeds to people overseas.

Across other networks, authorities describe money transmitters, online payment services and Chinese bank accounts carrying payments onward.

American facilitators can keep a share. Overseas recipients receive the transfers. U.S. authorities identify the North Korean state as the principal beneficiary.

The people whose names appear on the paychecks can receive something else: tax records for jobs they never held.

Authorities initially allege that Chapman’s operation creates false tax liabilities for more than 35 Americans. Much of the income is reported to federal tax and Social Security authorities under stolen or borrowed names.

Chapman pleads guilty in February 2025 to wire-fraud conspiracy, aggravated identity theft and money-laundering conspiracy.

In June, the Justice Department announces searches of 29 known or suspected laptop farms across 16 states, alongside seizures of financial accounts, fraudulent websites and about 200 computers.

Officials also describe a separate Atlanta-area case in which North Korean workers allegedly get jobs at a blockchain research company and steal more than $900,000 in virtual currency.

Chapman returns to court on July 24.

She receives 102 months in prison: eight and a half years.

The Justice Department puts her conspiracy’s revenue at more than $17.1 million for her and North Korea. It counts 68 compromised American identities, 309 defrauded U.S. businesses and two international businesses.

The employers include an aerospace manufacturer, an American automaker, a luxury retailer and a top-five television network, officials say.

Chapman has also shipped 49 company-supplied laptops and other devices abroad, including shipments to a Chinese city near North Korea’s border.

Her home is no longer hosting the operation. Other homes still are.

## The Next Applicant

A drug test requires a body, not just an online profile.

In November 2025, the Justice Department announces guilty pleas in schemes where American helpers supply that too. In one Georgia operation, two facilitators appear for drug tests on workers’ behalf.

Officials say that scheme produces about $1.28 million in salaries, most of which goes overseas. One facilitator, an active-duty U.S. Army member at the time, receives at least $51,397.

Didenko also pleads guilty that November. He later receives five years in prison for his identity-selling and laptop-farm operation.

More sentences follow in April 2026. American facilitators Kejia Wang and Zhenxing Wang receive 108 months and 92 months respectively.

According to the Justice Department, their scheme uses at least 80 stolen American identities, obtains jobs at more than 100 U.S. companies and generates more than $5 million for North Korea.

By October, authorities are searching again.

Operation Lonestar Roundup reaches more than 40 suspected laptop-farm residences in Texas. The Justice Department announces the seizure of approximately 187 laptops and remote-control devices.

The FBI’s warning that month describes an operation changing shape in response to stronger screening and law-enforcement pressure.

Fewer computers at more addresses. More paid American helpers. Borrowed professional accounts. People hired to interview, verify identities, provide fingerprints or keep a work device connected at home.

The FBI says financially stressed Americans can encounter fake job listings offering payment for apparently simple favors.

Receive a package. Host a laptop. Attend a meeting.

According to the bureau, teams typically consist of around a dozen military-aged men under group leaders who report upward into North Korean state organizations. The largest operator is likely the Munitions Industry Department, it says.

Its estimate is that the North Korean government receives 85 to 90 percent of the revenue.

The bureau also describes threats against former managers and their families, extortion involving stolen personal information, and facilitators hired to intimidate people in person.

The targets now include companies with government contracts and roles connected to security clearances. The FBI says workers are even seeking active-duty military members to host laptops.

Companies face a hiring process in which the résumé, interview, identity check and American delivery address can each involve a different person. The FBI recommends checks throughout hiring, onboarding and employment, rather than relying on one decisive test.

But the next application still arrives through an ordinary hiring system, for an ordinary job.

Who will actually do that work? Who will receive the paycheck? And will the employer discover the answers before the new hire gets access?
